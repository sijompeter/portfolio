Availability, discovered too late

Users were finding out whether a property was actually available only after they'd invested real effort in choosing it. The booking rate was the metric; the sequence was the problem.

RoleProduct Designer. End-to-end product design for key customer journeys.
OwnedBooking journey, search and discovery, UX audit, the resident app, iOS, Android and web.
TeamProduct and engineering, with operations as a standing constraint on supply.
TimelineWithin Dec 2019 – Apr 2021. [Exact window]

This case study is in progress

Same structure as case study 01. Each dashed box says what belongs in it and, where it matters, what makes the difference between a convincing answer and a vague one. The slots are empty on purpose, a fabricated research finding at a real company is the one thing on a portfolio you can't defend when someone asks.

Context

A product with two users who never meet

Zolo is a co-living platform: managed shared housing, taken for months at a time. Most of my time there went to the customer-facing side, discovery, listings, booking and the resident app, across mobile, web and PWAs.

I also designed the internal property and user-management dashboards the operations team ran on, which is the part that makes this project interesting. Booking is where the two sides collide: the resident wants certainty before committing, and operations needs a confirmed booking before holding a bed. Having designed both ends, I knew what each one was willing to give up.

The problem

Where the bookings were being lost

"Improve the booking rate" is a business goal, not a problem statement. The work is turning one into the other.

What I found, then the slot

Users discovered property availability too late in the process. They invested effort choosing a place, then hit a wall. I surfaced availability earlier in search, so people could make a better decision before entering the deeper stages of the booking funnel.

Still to add from your own records:

  • The stage with the biggest fall, stated as a proportion rather than a raw count
  • What tool told you, analytics, session recordings, sales call notes, ops complaints
  • What the baseline booking rate was, if you can state it

Slot, why it mattered commercially

An empty bed costs money every night it stays empty, and a booking that falls through after a bed is held costs twice. Write the version that's true for Zolo's model.

Upload, the old flow

Screens from the booking experience as it was. The before material is what makes the after material mean anything, and it's the thing people most often fail to keep. If the originals are gone, say so on the frame rather than rebuilding them unlabelled.

Research

What prospective residents told me

What I found, then the slot

15+ usability issues surfaced in a UX audit across navigation, visual hierarchy, interaction patterns and empty states. The recommendations were adopted and shipped. Lead with that count, an audit whose findings actually ship is rarer than it sounds.

Still to add:

  • How many people did you talk to, and who were they, residents, drop-offs, sales staff?
  • What were they actually afraid of? With housing it's rarely price alone.
  • Did ops or sales already know something the product didn't?

Slot, assumed / found / changed

The three-beat structure:

  • What I assumed, probably something about price or form length
  • What I found, the real hesitation, in their words
  • What changed, the restated problem, one sentence

If sales or ops interviews were part of this, say so. Internal research is still research, and on a two-sided product it's often where the answer is.

The design

What I changed, and why

Slot, key decision 01

Problem → decision → why → result. On a booking flow the strongest decision is usually about what you let someone do before committing, reserve, hold, compare, visit, cancel freely. Reducing the size of the first commitment beats explaining it better.

Slot, key decision 02

Same structure, with a before/after if one exists. A good second candidate: what you did about the gap between booking online and the ops reality of actually moving in.

Slot, before / after

The step-count comparison from case study 01 works well here. Count the steps the resident had to manage in the old flow and in the new one, and make the chip list match the number you claim.

Edge states

When it doesn't go right

Upload + slot, edge state 01

No availability, or the room going while someone was deciding. The most likely real failure on an inventory product.

Upload + slot, edge state 02

A failed payment, an abandoned booking, a cancellation, or a move-in date that moves. Pick the two you designed.
