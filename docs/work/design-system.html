04 — marketfeed · Design system · Adopted org-wide in 6 months

Keystone

marketfeed’s first shared design system, built from scratch by the only designer on the product, and adopted across the company within six months.

RoleSolo-built. Sole designer on the product throughout.
OwnedDesign tokens, reusable components, interaction patterns, documentation and Flutter specifications.
ConsumersThe whole company within six months. iOS and Android, built in Flutter. [Engineer count]
TimelineOrg-wide adoption inside six months, then maintained alongside the roadmap.

This case study is in progress

A design system case study fails differently from a product one: the trap is showing a colour grid and a button sheet and calling it a system. The slots below push toward the thing hiring managers actually want to see, adoption, governance, and what broke.

Context

Why a system existed at all

marketfeed moved from a financial education platform into automated trading, and the mobile app was built and rebuilt around that shift. One designer, several engineers, and a product that kept changing shape.

That is the honest case for a design system at this size: not consistency for its own sake, but a single person being the bottleneck on every screen in a product shipping faster than they could draw.

What I have, then the slot

Keystone shipped as tokens, reusable components, interaction patterns, documentation and Flutter specifications, so the system reached engineers as a build contract rather than a picture of one. That Flutter layer is the detail worth leading with: most design-system case studies stop at Figma.

Still to add:

  • What was there before, nothing, or an inherited mess?
  • What triggered it? A redesign, a platform change, a second product?
  • Which platforms consumed it, iOS, Android, React Native, web?
  • Was it Figma-only, or did it reach code?

The problem

What was actually going wrong

"We didn't have a design system" is not a problem. The problem is what its absence cost, in hours or in defects.

Slot, the evidence

Pick the symptoms you genuinely remember:

  • How many variants of the same component existed? Count them, that's a great number.
  • How long did a simple screen take from spec to build?
  • What kept coming back in QA because it was drawn slightly differently?
  • How often were engineers blocked waiting on you for a spacing value?

Upload, the before

An audit board: every button, every card, every input in the product before the system. This single image does more for a design system case study than any component sheet. If you ran that audit, it's the strongest asset you have.

Constraints

What I couldn't change

Resourcing

One designer

No design-systems team, no dedicated time, and every hour spent on the system was an hour not spent on the roadmap. State how you resolved that tension, it's the real story here.

Technical

[Constraint]

What the codebase, framework or native platforms imposed on naming, theming or token structure.

Product

[Constraint]

Brand, regulatory disclosure requirements, or a legacy product you couldn't migrate.

Scope, my call

[Constraint]

What you deliberately didn't systematise. A designer who knows what to leave bespoke reads as more senior than one who systematises everything.

The system

What it is made of

Show the parts, but lead with the decisions behind them. Anyone can show a colour ramp; the interesting question is why it has that many steps.

Slot, key decision 01

A naming or token-structure decision, with the reasoning. For a fintech product the strongest candidate is usually how money and numbers are typeset, tabular figures, the ₹ lakh/crore format, the colour rules for gain and loss, and how you handled the fact that colour alone can't carry that meaning accessibly.

Slot, key decision 02

Something about how the system was governed: who could add a component, what happened when an engineer needed something that didn't exist, how you stopped it rotting. This is the question senior design-system roles are actually screening for.

Adoption

Getting it used

A system nobody adopts is a Figma file. This section is what separates a real design-system case study from a style-guide screenshot.

Slot, how you rolled it out

  • Big-bang migration, or component by component as screens were touched?
  • How did you get engineering to use it, a shared library, a code package, a handoff convention?
  • What did you have to give up to get agreement?

Slot, how you kept it alive

  • Versioning and changelog, if any
  • Who reviewed additions
  • What happened when the company pivoted, did the system survive, and what had to be rebuilt?

Impact

What changed

Adoption

6 months

Org-wide, from nothing

The number that matters most for a system: not that it existed, but that everyone moved onto it.

Metric 02

[Figure]

Coverage, share of screens or components on the system. Or the variant count collapsing, which is the same story told backwards.

Scoping time

2 hrs → 20 min

With AI-assisted workflows

A system plus a prototyping workflow on top of it. Worth separating out which of the two did the work.

Design-system impact is often qualitative, and that's fine. "Engineers stopped asking me for spacing values" and "a new screen could be specced in an afternoon" are both legitimate, checkable outcomes. Write those rather than a made-up percentage, a hiring manager who has built a system will trust the honest version more.

Reflection

What I took from it

Slot, three learnings

Three. The best ones for a system are usually about restraint and about people, not about tokens.

Slot, one thing I'd change

A specific regret. Over-engineering something that was used twice is the most common and most relatable one, if that's true, say it.
